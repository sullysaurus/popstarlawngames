Accessible lawn games begin with the layout and rules, not a separate activity labeled for certain guests. Clear paths, firm surfaces, adjustable throwing lines, seated play options, and flexible team roles make the same event easier for more people to enjoy.

Ask guests about accommodations when appropriate and avoid assuming what someone can or cannot do.

## Choose a usable surface

Firm, level ground is easier for wheelchairs, walkers, canes, and anyone in formal shoes. Grass may look attractive but become difficult when soft, sloped, or wet. Patios and covered floors can support compact stations when the venue allows them.

Keep cords, bags, signs, and unused pieces out of travel paths.

## Leave room beside the station

The equipment footprint is not enough. A player needs space to approach, turn, wait, and leave without entering another lane. Spectators should have a defined area that does not narrow the accessible route.

Place at least one lower-impact game near seating, shade, and an accessible path from the main event.

## Adapt distance and roles

Offer more than one throwing line and let players toss from a seated position when the game permits. In team formats, rotate roles such as strategist, scorekeeper, caller, and thrower.

Do not require a guest to explain why they use a closer line. State at the beginning that players may choose the mark that fits them.

## Make instructions readable

Use high-contrast signs, large text, plain language, and a short demonstration. Do not rely on color alone to distinguish teams or scores. A host should be available for verbal instructions without rushing the player.

## Keep flexibility in the schedule

Long lines and timed elimination can make accommodations harder. Open play or generous station windows give guests time to participate comfortably. For a structured event, build transition time into every rotation.
