Good lawn games for seniors emphasize balance, visibility, and choice. Ring toss, giant four-in-a-row, yard dice, shortened-distance cornhole, and putting games can all work when the surface is stable and guests can play seated or take a lower-impact role.

Design for mixed abilities rather than treating age as a single physical category.

## Start with the surface

Use firm, level ground with a clear route from seating. Soft lawns, slopes, loose gravel, and wet grass can create more difficulty than the game itself. Remove spare pieces and bags from paths.

Put compact stations near shade, water, and chairs so a guest can rest without leaving the social area.

## Offer adjustable distances

Mark a closer line for tossing and putting. Let every player select the distance that works without requiring an explanation. For team play, rotate the thrower, strategist, scorer, and caller.

The goal is meaningful participation, not enforcing one physical standard.

## Make games easy to see and understand

Use high-contrast equipment and large-print rules. Demonstrate one complete turn. Avoid fast simultaneous play at adjacent stations, which can make it difficult to follow whose turn it is.

Four-in-a-row and ring toss have clear visual goals and short rounds. Cornhole can be comfortable at a reduced distance.

## Avoid long waits

Open play lets guests choose a quiet moment. In a rotation, use small teams and enough time for unhurried turns. Provide a chair at or near stations where the venue permits.

## Ask rather than assume

If the event has known access requirements, share them with the venue and equipment provider. Do not exclude a guest because of age or decide in advance that someone needs help.
