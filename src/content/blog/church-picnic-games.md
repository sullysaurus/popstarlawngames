Church picnic games should make it easy for children, teenagers, parents, and older adults to share the same activity area. Cornhole, giant four-in-a-row, ring toss, yard dice, and a giant tower work well because leaders can adjust distances and keep rounds short.

## Create a welcoming mix

Use one throwing game, one strategy game, one luck-based game, and a seated or shaded option. Avoid building the whole event around speed or strength. Mixed-age teams can help newcomers join without feeling singled out.

Keep participation optional. A clear “open play” sign is often more inviting than repeated announcements asking everyone to compete.

## Add a short group challenge

If the picnic needs a shared moment, schedule a 20-minute family relay, cornhole final, or team station rotation. Publish the time and rules beforehand so guests can choose whether to play or watch.

Award points for participation and teamwork as well as wins. Small, lighthearted prizes keep the focus on community.

## Plan for children and older guests

Mark closer throwing lines for children and place lower-impact games near chairs and shade. Use large-print rules and keep paths suitable for strollers and mobility devices.

Adults should supervise giant equipment and resets. Separate throwing lanes from playground exits, buffet lines, and parking areas.

## Prepare for weather

Identify a covered backup area and decide which compact games can move. Hot-weather plans should include water, shade, and a shorter activity window. Stop play when wet ground or wind makes equipment unstable.

## Assign game-area ownership

One volunteer should know the setup, rules, first-aid location, and event timeline. Rotate helpers so no one spends the entire picnic managing equipment.
