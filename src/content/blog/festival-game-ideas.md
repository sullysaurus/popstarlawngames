Festival games need faster resets and clearer boundaries than backyard-party games. Cornhole, giant four-in-a-row, ring toss, yard dice, and putting challenges can work when each station has a visible entry point, a short turn, and enough room for a queue.

## Plan for throughput

Estimate the busiest hour rather than total attendance. A game that serves four people every ten minutes cannot absorb a long line without duplicates or a shorter format.

Use fixed attempts—three rings, three bags, or three putts—when the crowd is heavy. Save full games for quieter periods or a scheduled final.

## Make the station readable

Guests should understand where to stand, what to do, and where to exit. Use one rules sign, a marked throwing line, and a separate waiting area. Avoid decorative copy that hides the actual instructions.

Number stations if the festival uses a passport or prize system.

## Separate different traffic types

Do not aim throws toward vendor tents, food lines, stages, rides, or entrance gates. Leave room for spectators without blocking circulation. Service vehicles and emergency routes must remain completely clear.

## Staff for resets and safety

High-use stations need someone to return pieces, answer questions, and pause play when a lane is crossed. Rotate staff and provide shade and water. Inspect the surface and equipment throughout the event, especially after weather changes.

## Decide whether prizes are necessary

Open play can be enough. If prizes are part of the event, use a consistent scoring rule, limit attempts, and explain tie breaks. Avoid valuable prizes that turn a casual station into a dispute.
