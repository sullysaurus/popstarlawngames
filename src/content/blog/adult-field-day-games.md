A good adult field day mixes accuracy, strategy, luck, and light movement. Cornhole, ring toss, putterball, giant four-in-a-row, yard dice, and a giant tower create competition without requiring everyone to sprint, crawl, or wear athletic clothes.

The format should make participation easy and opting out comfortable.

## Choose balanced stations

Avoid six versions of throwing hard or running fast. A balanced lineup might include:

- Cornhole for familiar head-to-head play
- Putterball for precision
- Four-in-a-row for quick strategy
- Yard dice for luck
- Ring toss for a compact accuracy station
- A giant tower for a team challenge

If you add a relay, offer a lower-impact role such as scorekeeper, caller, or puzzle solver.

## Build teams fairly

Teams of four to eight are large enough to create energy but small enough for everyone to get a turn. Random assignment usually works better than department-versus-department when groups differ in size or physical ability.

Use color bands or printed team cards and assign a starting station. Publish the schedule before the first whistle so the host is not reorganizing the crowd in real time.

## Set a rotation that can recover

Allow roughly 10 to 15 minutes per station plus transition time. Include one open or break station where teams can get water, use the restroom, or catch up after a slow round. Without that buffer, one rules question can delay the whole event.

Score each activity on the same scale—perhaps five points for first, three for second, and one for completion—rather than mixing raw scores that cannot be compared.

## Keep the rules visible

Each station needs one short rule card, the scoring method, and a clear reset instruction. A host should demonstrate any unfamiliar activity before rotations begin. For a casual office social, open play may be better than a scored field day; see our [corporate event lawn game guide](/blog/corporate-event-lawn-games/).

## End cleanly

Set the final round before people become hungry or tired. Announce results promptly, keep prizes light, and recognize something beyond first place—best teamwork or best team name works well.
